Complete Graphs and Colorings

The complete graph Kn is the graph with n vertices and an edge between every pair of vertices. Pictured to the right are the complete graphs K3, K4, K5, K6. Notice that Kn "contains" copies of Km, where m<n, i.e. smaller complete graphs. For example, the graph K4 contains many copies of the graph K3.

Suppose we split the edges into two groups. We've done this, for example, in the graphs of K5, K6 to the right by coloring the edges in one group red and the edges in the other group blue. Now we can ask: Must the graph contain a K3 all of whose edges are the same color? (If you look for such a graph in the graphs to the right, it will appear to be a triangle whose sides are all red or all blue.) Before we answer this question, we'll define some terminology.

For our purposes a coloring of a graph is a drawing of the graph with the edges colored. If the coloring uses only one color, we says it is monochromatic. If the coloring uses k colors, we call it a k-colorings. The graphs to the right of K5 and K6 are 2-colorings.

Using this terminology, the question we are interested in answering is: Is there a number N such that for any n ≥ N, every 2-coloring of Kn contains a monochromatic K3 (triangle)? And further, what is the smallest such N? The answer to this question is known as the Ramsey number, R(3, 3). Since the 2-coloring of K5 to the right does not contain a red K3 or a blue K3, we see that R(3, 3) cannot be 5, so R(3, 3) ≥ 6.

Ramsey Number R(3, 3) = 6

To see why, it is helpful to consider a 2-coloring of K5 not containing a monochromatic triangle (for example, the coloring above). Notice that in that 2-coloring, at every vertex there are two edges of each color. This must be the case in any coloring that does not contain a monochromatic triangle. Why is that?

Consider a vertex thst has three (or more) edges of the same color. Why does this force there to be a monochromatic triangle? In the diagram to the right, vertex A has three red edges. The edges drawn as dashed lines must be red or blue edges. If one is red, then that edge and two edges to vertex A will be a red triangle. On the other hand if all dashed lines are blue, then they themselves form a blue triangle. Either way, we will have a monochromatic triangle.

To prove that R(3, 3) = 6 then it will be enough to prove that a 2-coloring of K6 contains a vertex with three or more edges of the same color. Consider the vertex B in the graph to the right. In K6, there are five edges (drawn dashed) at vertex B. At least three of those edge must be the same color (otherwise there would be at most two red edges and at most two blue edges which would account for only four of the colored edges at B).

Sim

Sim (named after mathematician Gustavus Simmons) is game that is guaranteed to not end in a tie by the fact that R(3, 3) = 6! Sim is a two player game played on K6. The players take turns drawing edges of the graph, each player using a different color. A player loses if he draws an edge that completes a K3, a monochromatic triangle of his color. Ramsey's result guarantees that by the time all edges are drawn, at least one player will have lost.

At the right you can play a game of Sim against an automated bot. You will move first and draw red edges. The bot will move second and draw blue edges. To draw an edge click on one vertex of the edge and drag to the other vertex of the edge. To win you must add edges without creating a red triangle. If successful, you will win when the bot creates a blue triangle.

To play again, visit the Sim gym where you can also play on K5.

TAG

TAG (Triangle Avoidance Game), like Sim, is a two player game played on a K6. The key difference is that on a player's turn the player chooses which color for the edge she draws. Like Sim, a player loses if she draws an edge that completes a monochromatic K3, i.e. a triangle (which will be of the color of the edge the player just drew). Again, Ramsey's result guarantees that one player or the other will lose, and thus the game will have a winner.

At the right you can play a game of Tag against a bot. You will draw the first edge. To choose the color you draw, click on the colored dot in the top left corner to toggle between the two colors. To win you must add edges without creating a monochromatic triangle. If successful, you will win when the bot creates a monochromatic triangle.

The Laboratory

The laboratory lets you play Sim and TAG on K5 or K6 and against a human or three different levels of bots: expert, intermediate, and beginner. Since Ramsey's theorem does not guarantee a monochromatic triangle on K5 Sim and TAG can end in a tie (but assuming best play by both players, does it?). The expert bot plays an optimal strategy and will, if possible, win from the starting position. The intermediate bot moves randomly up to about 1/3 of the game, and then plays optimally for the remainder of the game. The beginner bot moves randomly for about 2/3 of the game, and then plays optimally.

In addition, the laboratory adds an undo button and a "show winning/losing moves" button. The undo button undoes the last move made by a human player. Thus, if two humans are playing, it usodes the last move. If a human is playing against a bot, it will undo the bot's last move and the human's last move.

The show winning/losing moves button toggles whether the display shows all winning and losing moves. When enabled, winning moves are shown as thin solid lines. Losing moves are shown as thin dashed lines. The color of the line indicates the move color. If there is no line of a color between vertices, drawing an edge of that color is tie. Due to Ramsey's result, this can only occur in games on K5.

In the case of TAG, since a player can choose either color, a line may have two colors, half one color and half the other color, and maybe half dashed, half solid. For example, in the TAG position shown to the right, the line from 0 to 3 is half solid blue, incdicating that drawing a blue edge is a winning move, and half dashed red, indicating that drawing a red edge is a losing move. The line between 0 and 5 is solid but half red and half blue, indicating that drawing either either a red or blue edge is a winning move. If only one color appears on a line, it indicates tht drawing an edge of the other color will result in a tie.
